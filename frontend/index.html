Pipeline

How it works

Four stages, from a satellite pass over a field to an alert in somebody’s hand. Nothing is simulated: every index is reduced from real imagery over a real boundary.

01 Observe

Sentinel-2 optical and Sentinel-1 radar passes are reduced over each field’s own geometry in Google Earth Engine. A fully cloud-masked scene is dropped rather than filled in — an absence of observation is not an observation of nothing.

02 Measure

Five vegetation and moisture indices per observation — NDVI, EVI, NDWI, SAVI and NBR — composited across the crop’s own growing window, then joined to district soil and weather.

03 Predict

A 500-tree Random Forest maps those indices to yield in maunds per acre. The spread across individual trees is reported as the interval, so a confident and an uncertain prediction do not look alike.

04 Warn

Each field’s curve is compared against its own history. A turn consistent with water or heat stress raises an alert while there is still a season left to act in.

Capabilities

Inside the platform

Eight screens on the farmer side, fifteen on the government side, over one Postgres database with row-level security deciding who sees which row.

Vegetation time series

Per-date NDVI, EVI, NDWI, SAVI and NBR for every registered field, with the radar channels beside them and the sensor named on each observation.

Field-level mapping

Leaflet over satellite basemaps, with each farm drawn as the circle the indices were actually reduced over — not a pin standing in for a boundary.

Harvest-window detection

The post-peak decline in a field’s own NDVI curve, used to flag a likely cut. Shown to growers as an indication, and deliberately not aggregated into an official statistic until it is validated.

Risk & stress alerts

Drought, water-stress and heat signals raised per field and rolled up per district, with the triggering observation attached so an officer can check the reasoning.

District analytics

Production and yield statistics, crop comparison, subsidy targeting by water retention, and CSV export — across 34 districts and eight reported seasons.

Field survey returns

District officers submit ground observations, scoped by the database to their own district and their own user id. Those returns are what will eventually validate the harvest detector.

The model, and how far to trust it

The production model is a median-composite Random Forest, held to a fixed holdout of two unseen seasons. Both the headline figure and the places it falls apart are published here, because a yield number a government acts on should arrive with its own error bars.

Why the 0.92 is not an accuracy claim. Pooled across eleven crops whose yields differ by an order of magnitude, R² mostly measures the model’s ability to tell sugarcane from wheat — not its skill within either. Per crop it ranges from +0.77 down to −0.72, and five of eleven crops score below zero, meaning the model does worse than predicting that crop’s own average. Every row is a district average, so this is not field-level accuracy either. Wheat is the one crop whose result survives a time-aware split, and it is the crop the project claims.

Provenance

Where the data comes from

Four sources, each with a named origin. Nothing on this platform is synthetic — where a figure does not exist, the screen that would show it says so instead of filling the gap.

LayerSourceResolution / grain
Optical imagery Sentinel-2 surface reflectance via Google Earth Engine 10 m, per satellite pass
Radar imagery Sentinel-1 C-band SAR (VV / VH) via Google Earth Engine Per pass, cloud-independent
Reported yields Crop Reporting Service final estimates, Punjab District × crop × season
Soil & weather Per-district soil profile and seasonal temperature series District, seasonal aggregate
Two screens in the government portal are deliberately empty. Crop-area estimation and harvest progress have no source data yet, so they render their full layout and name the missing input rather than displaying a plausible number. A missing value prints as an em dash, never as zero, and a district with no reading is drawn grey on the map rather than at the bottom of the colour ramp.
Architecture

Technology

Three parts: a browser front end that talks to Postgres directly for plain reads and writes, a Python service that owns everything needing Earth Engine or scikit-learn, and the database that enforces access.

Front end
React 18 · Vite · Tailwind v4 · Leaflet
ML service
FastAPI · scikit-learn · Google Earth Engine · 10 endpoints
Database
Supabase · PostgreSQL · PostGIS · row-level security
Government schema
13 tables · 4 helper functions · 24 RLS policies

Anything requiring Python lives in the ML service; anything that is a plain table read goes straight to Postgres from the browser, so no CRUD API exists that nobody needed. The service’s privileged key never reaches the browser bundle.

Security

Who can see what

Access is decided by the database, not by the interface. Hiding a button is a presentation choice; a row-level security policy is the thing that actually stops a request.

Farmers see only their own farms

Enforced by a policy on the farms table keyed to the signed-in user, so a crafted request returns nothing rather than someone else’s field.

Three government tiers

A super admin sees the province; a district manager sees one district and its staff; an employee sees their district in the role they were given. A manager cannot read another district.

No self-promotion

There is no update policy letting anyone change their own tier or district — not even the administrator. The escalation is absent, not merely checked for.

The government portal is read-only

Yield predictions, satellite indices, risk alerts and subsidy recommendations carry no write policy at any tier, so no official can edit a figure the model produced.

No individual farm records cross over

No role in the government portal can read a single row of the farmers’ table. Officials see district aggregates; growers keep their own data.

57 boundary checks, all passing

A verification script creates throwaway accounts across two districts, signs in as a browser would, and asserts every boundary from both sides — that the right rows are visible and the wrong ones are not.

Questions

Frequently asked

Why do I need a separate login for each portal?

Because the three audiences have incompatible access rules. A farmer account is self-registered and scoped to its own fields; a government account is issued by an administrator and scoped to a district. Sharing one sign-in would mean one account could hold both shapes, which is exactly the escalation the schema is built to prevent.

Can I get a government account?

Not by signing up — the portal has no registration form at all. Accounts are created by a super admin, who issues district managers; each manager then creates the officers and analysts in their own district.

Is the yield prediction accurate enough to act on?

For wheat, it is the result the project stands behind: R² around 0.72 on unseen seasons, and it holds up under a time-aware split where the model never sees the future. For five of the eleven crops it currently scores below zero, which means it is worse than guessing that crop’s average, and the portal says so rather than averaging it away. Treat anything outside wheat as indicative.

Why is the prediction a district average rather than my field?

The only yields available to train on are the Crop Reporting Service’s district figures, so every training row is a district average. The satellite indices are measured over your own field boundary, which is what makes a per-field monitoring signal meaningful — but the yield scale behind it is district-level, and calling it field-level accuracy would overstate the evidence.

What happens when clouds cover my field?

That date is dropped from the optical series rather than filled in, so a gap in the chart is a real gap in observation. Sentinel-1 radar flies a different orbit and sees through cloud, so many dates still carry radar readings with no vegetation index — those rows show the radar values and leave NDVI empty rather than inventing one.

Which crops and districts are covered?

Thirty-four districts of Punjab, and eleven crops: wheat, rice, maize, cotton, sugarcane, barley, bajra, jowar, potato, onion and tomato. The district list is the one the pipeline actually fetched data for, so no selector offers a district whose charts would come back empty.
